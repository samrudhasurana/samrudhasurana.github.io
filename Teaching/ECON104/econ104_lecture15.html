Business Fluctuations

  • We can see the booms and recessions in the US economy by quarter from 1960 onwards.
  • The average rate of growth of real GDP is 3.1% per quarter (on an annual basis), but the economy rarely grew at an average rate. In a typical recession, the growth rate might drop to −5% in some quarters, and in a boom, the economy can grow at a rate of 7% to 8% or higher.
  • We call the fluctuations of real GDP around its long-term trend or “normal” growth rate business fluctuations or business cycles.
  • Recessions are of concern because they are associated with unemployment.

What happens during a recession

  • Resources of all kinds — land, labor, and capital are not fully employed.
  • During a recession, factories close, stores are boarded up, and farmland is left fallow.
  • More generally, when there are a lot of unemployed resources, it suggests that resources are being wasted and the economy is operating below its potential.

Recall the “Equation of Exchange”

Mv = PY

Note: Y is real GDP (real output), even though we are dropping the subscript used on the earlier slides.

We can now write a growth rate version of this equation,

M+v=P+Y
M
growth rate of money supply
v
growth rate of velocity
P
growth rate of price level (INFLATION)
Y
growth rate of real GDP (REAL GROWTH)

ECON 104
13

Back to aggregate demand

  • We can think of aggregate demand as spending in the economy.
  • M + v is the spending.
  • The Aggregate Demand curve shows ALL the possible combinations of inflation (P) and real GDP growth (Y) that can happen for a GIVEN rate of spending growth (M + v) in the entire economy.
ECON 104
14

The spending pie

M+v=P+Y
  • Think of total spending in the economy as a pie.
  • This “spending pie” has to be divided between two things:
    • More stuff: an increase in the actual goods and services we produce (Y)
    • Higher prices: an increase in the price level (P)
ECON 104
15

Spending: more stuff or higher prices?

  • If you’re spending $2,000 per month now and then spend $3,000 next month — you might be spending more on either buying more things or paying higher prices for the same things.
  • If money growth = 5%, velocity = 0%, and real growth is 0%, the inflation rate = 5%.
  • Another example: If money growth = 5%, velocity = 0%, and real growth is 3%, the inflation rate = 2%.
ECON 104
16

The aggregate demand curve

  • An AD curve tells us all the combinations of inflation and real growth that are consistent with a specified rate of spending growth.
  • Let’s say money growth + velocity growth (M + v) = 5%.
  • The AD curve slopes downward.
  • An AD curve with a slope of −1 means a 1 percentage point increase in real growth reduces inflation by 1 percentage point.
ECON 104
17

Shifts in the Aggregate Demand Curve

  • Increased spending must flow into either a higher inflation rate or a higher growth rate.
    • If spending growth increases, because of either an increase in money supply or an increase in velocity, then the AD curve shifts up and to the right.
  • A decrease in spending growth shifts the AD curve inward.

Examples of AD shocks

  1. 2008 Financial Crisis: Due to fear of loss of jobs and value of their homes, people reduced spending. AD curve shifted to the left.
  2. Covid-19 spending package: In response to the COVID-19 pandemic, the U.S. government passed several large stimulus packages, sending checks to households and increasing government spending. This injection of spending into the economy caused the AD curve to shift to the right. The goal was to boost real growth, but as the model predicts, it also led to higher inflation.

Long-Run Aggregate Supply Curve

ECON 104
22

The Long-Run Aggregate Supply Curve

  • Supply is determined by the availability of factors of production.
  • While relative price changes move production factors from one firm or industry to another, for the economy as a whole, the total amount of factors are given:
    • The amount of capital (factories, tools, computers, etc.)
    • The size and skill of the labor force (human capital)
    • The state of technology and ideas (R&D, innovation)
ECON 104
23

The Long-Run Aggregate Supply Curve

These real factors determine the economy’s potential real GDP growth rate, not inflation. THIS IS WHY THE CURVE IS VERTICAL. No amount of inflation can change this long-run growth trajectory.
ECON 104
24

The Long-Run Aggregate Supply Curve

Once more (this is important!):

  • Whether the inflation rate is 0%, 2%, or 200%, this does not change the number of factories, the education of the workforce, or the development of new ideas.
  • That is why the curve is vertical.
ECON 104
25

Putting them together

  • This AD curve shows an annual growth rate in spending of 10% per year.
  • Spending is going up 10% every year, but how much of that is inflation and how much is real GDP growth?
  • Well, the economy can only grow at 3% per year according to our LRAS curve.
ECON 104
26

Putting them together

  • That means that the rate of inflation must be 7%.
  • The equilibrium growth rate of real GDP is 3% per year and the inflation rate is 7% per year, while spending grows at 10% per year.

Shifts in the LRAS curve: real shocks!

ECON 104
28

Real Shocks to the LRAS

  • A positive real shock shifts the LRAS curve to the right
    • The real GDP growth rate rises
    • Inflation rate falls
    • Same money spending chasing more goods → prices rise more slowly
  • A negative real shock shifts the LRAS curve to the left
    • The real GDP growth rate falls
    • Inflation rate rises
    • Same money spending chasing fewer goods → prices rise more quickly
  • This view of explaining business fluctuations, based on the LRAS is called the Real Business Cycle Theory (we’ll revisit this later).
ECON 104
29

Real Shock: Oil

  • Think about the most recent shock to the US economy!
  • In an economy with a large manufacturing sector, a reduction in the oil supply reduces GDP.
  • Oil and machines are complementary: They work together with labor to produce output.
  • When the oil supply is reduced, capital and labor become less productive.
  • The first OPEC oil shock came in late 1973, and the price of oil more than tripled in two years.
