Recap

M+v=P+Y

The growth-rate form of the equation of exchange, Mv = PY.

  • Aggregate demand is spending in the economy, M + v. The AD curve shows ALL the possible combinations of inflation (P) and real GDP growth (Y) for a GIVEN rate of spending growth.
  • GDP growth is not smooth. Some years have high real GDP growth and some may have low (or even negative) real GDP growth.
  • In the long-run, REAL SHOCKS (LRAS curve shifts) affect the output growth rate AND inflation growth rate.
  • In the long-run, aggregate demand shocks (AD curve shifts) affect only inflation.

Supply creates its own demand

Say’s Law, as John Maynard Keynes summarized it.

“From the time of Say and Ricardo the classical economists have taught that supply creates its own demand …”

— John Maynard Keynes, The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936), ch. 2
  • Producing goods creates the income needed to buy goods.
  • Markets adjust themselves, so we should not expect lasting slack in the economy.

Jean-Baptiste Say

What Say actually meant: you cannot have demand without supply.

So why do we see slack?

If markets adjust themselves, how can an economy produce below its potential?

  • People can sell goods and then hold the cash instead of spending it, so spending falls.
  • Prices and wages do not adjust right away, so output and jobs adjust instead.
Money and sticky prices are what break Say’s Law in the short run.

Short-Run Aggregate Supply Curve

ECON 104

Short-Run Aggregate Supply (SRAS) Curve

  • If real GDP growth rate is fixed in the long-run at some constant rate, why does real GDP growth fluctuate so much from year to year?
  • Why don’t we just have consistent growth at that level?
  • Because in the short-run, the aggregate supply curve is not vertical — it slopes up.
Short-run aggregate supply (SRAS) curve

A curve that shows the positive relationship between the inflation rate and real growth during the period when prices and wages are sticky.

A parable about money-printing

  • Imagine the government prints more money and gives it to soldiers.
  • The soldiers use the money to buy goods.
  • This new spending shifts out the AD curve.
  • Imagine you’re a storeowner — how would you respond to this increased spending?
ECON 104
13

You’d produce more!

  • You put in extra work; buy new equipment; hire new employees temporarily.
  • Real GDP growth rate increases!
  • You have higher income now and can spend in other places.
ECON 104
14

BUT, what do you find?

  • Prices in other places have gone up!
  • Your own production costs have increased.
  • Soon, your employees (also facing higher prices) demand higher wages.
  • The increased level of output is no longer profitable… you increase your prices (inflation rate increases) and reduce production back to the earlier rate (real GDP growth rate falls back to the long-run level).
ECON 104
15

The key question

The key question is: why are people responding by increasing production instead of increasing prices by the level of increase in money?
ECON 104
16

AD shock in the short-run and the long-run

  • The increase in AD is driven by the newly created money.
  • Growth rate of the money supply (gM) increases, shifting out the aggregate demand curve.
  • Because the worker is seeing increased demand for her product but wages haven’t had time to rise yet, she increases her prices and increases output.
    • This is the movement from point a to point b.
  • The inflation rate the shopkeeper is expecting is still the old long-run inflation rate of 2%.
  • The increase of spending (5%) is split between increases in inflation (2%) and the real GDP growth rate (3%).
ECON 104
17

AD shock in the short-run and the long-run

ECON 104
18

Sticky wages

  • An important part of the story is that when increased demand for the product occurs, the storeowner can hire new employees at the same wages.
  • The wages haven’t increased yet!
  • The shopkeeper can raise the price of her goods, increase her output, and earn a profit because her employees aren’t yet demanding higher wages. This is why production increases in the short run.
  • But after a while, the employees (facing higher prices everywhere) will start demanding higher wages. This increases the shopkeeper’s costs, and forces her to cut back on production and raise prices further.
ECON 104
19

Sticky wages

  • When employees see prices everywhere rising, their real wage, the real amount of goods and services that they can purchase, is declining.
  • But importantly, they do not notice this immediately. This is called “nominal wage confusion,” because they confuse higher nominal wages (overtime pay) with higher real wages (increased purchasing power).
  • They are happy to work longer hours for a little pay bump — this is what allows output to increase. However, they soon realize that the overtime pay isn’t covering the higher prices for all goods and services and begin demanding higher wages.
ECON 104
20

Sticky prices

  • Prices also don’t seem to change immediately, because
    1. It is costly — menu costs! Plus, customers may be unhappy.
    2. Because firms aren’t sure whether the increase in demand is temporary or permanent, they may hold off on changing prices.
  • Because the increase in spending growth must translate into either an increase in inflation or an increase in the real GDP growth rate AND PRICES AND WAGES ARE STICKY, real GDP growth rates increase in the short run.
ECON 104
21

Back to the long-run

ECON 104
22

Back to the long-run

In the long-run:

  • Employees demand higher wages.
  • They are less willing to work overtime consistently.
  • Shopkeepers realize that the increase in prices is not due to a change in consumer tastes for their product but instead is due to an increase in the money supply.
  • They realize the increase in the price level is permanent — they might as well change their menus.
ECON 104
23

Back to the long-run

  • Wages and prices continue to rise.
  • Real GDP growth rates decline.
  • The economy moves from point b to point c back to the long-run equilibrium.
ECON 104
24

Back to the long-run

  • In the end, the increase in gM is reflected entirely in a higher inflation rate.
  • Once again, in the long-run, money is neutral — it does not affect the real GDP growth rate. In the long-run the economy MUST be on the LRAS curve.
  • However money does affect the inflation rate, which is now higher.
ECON 104

Negative AD Shock

ECON 104
26

Fall in AD

Going the other way, now consider a decline in the growth rate of the money supply.

  1. Spending declines and shopkeepers see a fall in demand for the products.
  2. Wages, however, are sticky and do not fall immediately.
  3. Shopkeepers therefore employ less labor and cut back on production, so real growth rates decline.
  4. This is a recession.
  5. Eventually wages (and therefore costs) become unstuck, and as costs fall, production picks back up.
  6. The economy returns to its long-run equilibrium at point c with an identical growth rate and a lower inflation rate.
ECON 104
27

Fall in AD

ECON 104
28

Wages and prices are especially sticky downward

Many economists think that declines in aggregate demand can bring about lengthy recessions because prices and wages are ESPECIALLY sticky in the downward direction.

  • Nobody likes to have their wages cut.
  • Cutting wages is bad for morale.
    • Easier to fire workers than cut their wages.
  • Many workers have long-term contracts that can’t be immediately adjusted.
But prices are falling, employers have to cut costs somewhere.
ECON 104
29

Shocks to Components of AD

Changes in v:

  • Are the same as changes in the spending rate, holding M constant.
  • Can be broken down into changes in the growth rate of C, I, G, or NX.
  • Changes in v tend to be temporary.
  • The shares of GDP devoted to C, I, G, and NX have been quite stable over time.

Stabilization!

  • There is an argument by some economists that when changes in investor or consumer confidence cause a fall in aggregate demand, government should increase spending (increase G) or increase the money supply (increase M) to shift aggregate demand back out and keep the economy on its long-run path.
  • We will talk more about stabilization in later lectures.
  • For now, just remember that declines in V (brought about by changes in C, I, or G) tend to be short-lived. The whole reason to have money is to spend it on something!

Takeaway

  • The aggregate demand and supply model can be used to analyze fluctuations in the growth rate of real GDP.
  • Real shocks are analyzed through shifts in the LRAS curve, while aggregate demand shocks are analyzed using shifts in the AD curve.
  • Nominal wage and price confusion, sticky wages and prices, menu costs, and uncertainty create an upward-sloping short-run aggregate supply curve.
ECON 104
41

Takeaway

  • The Great Depression and the COVID-19 recession were created by a combination of aggregate demand and real shocks.
  • When you combine the aggregate demand curve, long-run aggregate supply curve, and short-run aggregate supply curve into a single diagram, you can analyze a wide variety of economic scenarios and how they affect the growth rate of the economy.
