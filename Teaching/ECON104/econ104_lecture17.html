Why Do Economies Fluctuate?

  • In the AD–AS model, real GDP growth fluctuates when AD shifts, when LRAS shifts, or when SRAS adjusts.
  • Economists disagree about which of these matters most.
  • Three schools of thought: monetarists, Keynesians, and real business cycle theorists.

Monetarists: Money Drives the Cycle

  • Associated with Milton Friedman (University of Chicago).
  • Fluctuations in real GDP growth come mainly from changes in the growth rate of the money supply.
  • Faster money growth shifts AD right; slower money growth shifts AD left.
  • In the long run, money is neutral. In the short run, sticky wages and prices let money growth move real GDP growth.

Monetarists: The Great Depression

  • Friedman and Schwartz, A Monetary History of the United States: the Great Depression was caused by a collapse in the money supply.
  • From 1929 to 1933 the U.S. money supply fell by about one-third as banks failed and the Federal Reserve did not offset it.
  • Less money → lower AD → deep recession.
  • Policy: a steady, predictable rate of money growth, set by a rule rather than by discretion.

Keynesians: Demand Drives the Cycle

  • Based on John Maynard Keynes’s General Theory (1936), written during the Great Depression.
  • Fluctuations come mainly from swings in AD: consumption, investment, government spending, and net exports.
  • Investment depends on confidence — what Keynes called “animal spirits.”
  • Because wages and prices are sticky, a fall in demand is corrected slowly and the economy can stay below potential.

Keynesians: Fighting Recessions

  • The economy does not quickly fix a demand shortfall by itself, so policy should offset it.
  • Fiscal policy: more government spending or lower taxes raise AD.
  • Monetary policy: faster money growth and lower interest rates raise AD.
  • Example: the COVID-19 stimulus (Lecture 16).

Real Business Cycle Theory: Real Shocks

  • Developed by Finn Kydland and Edward Prescott (Nobel Prize, 2004).
  • Fluctuations come from real shocks: productivity and technology, energy prices, weather, disasters — not from money or demand.
  • Real shocks shift LRAS, so the economy’s growth rate itself rises and falls.
  • Wages and prices adjust quickly, so sticky prices are not needed to explain the cycle.
ECON 104
9

Real Business Cycle Theory: Recessions as Responses

  • After a negative productivity shock, working and investing pay less, so households and firms rationally cut back.
  • Shocks spread: the 2011 earthquake and tsunami in Japan disrupted sectors far from where it struck.
  • Policy: the economy is already responding efficiently, so stabilization policy has little to offer.
  • Critics: sticky wages and prices are real, and it is hard to find the shock behind the 2008 financial crisis.

Takeaway

  • Monetarists look to money growth and Keynesians look to spending and confidence: both see shifts in AD, with sticky wages and prices turning them into real GDP fluctuations.
  • Real business cycle theorists look to real shocks that shift LRAS.
  • Each school gives a different answer to “if real GDP falls, where should we look?” The AD–AS model lets us compare them.
