Federal Tax Revenues

  • As of 2025, the federal government takes in about $5 trillion per year.
  • That works out to nearly $15,000 for every person in the US.
  • Two sources account for more than 80% of the revenue:
    • Individual income tax
    • Social Security and Medicare taxes

Taxes

Marginal tax rate

The tax rate paid on an additional dollar of income.

Average tax rate

The total tax payment divided by total income.

ECON 104

Individual income tax

  • Individual income taxes are the single largest source of revenue for the federal government.
  • Marginal tax rates:
    • For incomes less than $23,850, the marginal tax rate is 10%.
    • For income earned greater than $751,600, the marginal tax rate is 37%.
    • In 1960, the lowest marginal tax rate was 20% and the highest rate was 91%.
ECON 104
8

Individual income tax

  • It is the marginal rate that determines things like the incentive to work additional hours.
  • If income = $50,000 you pay:
    • 10% on the first $23,850 = $2,385
    • 12% on the next $26,150 = $3,138
    • Average tax rate: $5,523/$50,000 × 100 = 11.05%.
  • Income received from salary, interest, dividends, and capital gains is also taxed.

Calculate Average tax rate

The tax rate is 10% on income up to $30,000 and 15% on income from $30,000 to $60,000. What is the average tax rate for someone earning $55,000?

  • 10% on the first $30,000 = $3,000
  • 15% on the next $25,000 = $3,750
  • Total tax = $6,750
Average tax rate = $6,750 / $55,000 × 100 = 12.27%

Social Security and Medicare Taxes

  • Almost all U.S. workers pay the Federal Insurance Contributions Act (FICA) tax, which funds Social Security payments.
  • The FICA tax is 6.2% of your wages up to a cap, and employers pay a 6.2% tax on the same earnings.
  • Medicare: Employers and Workers pay 1.45% each. Self-employed individuals pay the full 2.9% themselves.
  • Research shows that much of the burden of the FICA tax falls on workers, not on employers.
  • The employer’s payment is mostly taken out of the worker’s prospective wage.

Corporate Income tax

  • The U.S. corporate income tax is 21% — one of the highest rates in the world.
  • Every dollar of corporate tax revenue ultimately comes from consumers (through higher prices), workers (through lower wages), or investors (through lower returns).
  • A corporate tax cut attracts more investment, which increases demand for labor and output! Workers benefit from corporate tax cuts.

State and Local Taxes

  • In addition to federal taxes, most people pay state and local taxes.
  • States raise more of their revenues – about 20%, on average – from sales taxes.
  • Since everyone pays sales taxes regardless of income, state and local taxation as a whole is less progressive than income taxation.

Social Security

  • Social Security is the single largest government program in the world, in terms of dollars paid.
  • The Social Security program pays more than $1 trillion to more than 70 million beneficiaries.
  • Current payments are paid out of current taxes.
  • The average retired worker receives $2,000 per month.
ECON 104

Medicare, Medicaid, and Unemployment Insurance

Medicare and Medicaid
  • Social Security and Medicare together are the largest undertakings of the U.S. government.
  • In 2025, Medicare spending amounted to nearly $1 trillion.
  • Medicare covers people over the age of 65, while Medicaid covers the poor and the disabled.
Unemployment Insurance
  • Unemployment insurance (UI) makes payments to people who are out of work; it is not restricted to the poor.
  • UI is a large program that can expand rapidly during recessions.
  • In 2021 and 2022, for example, this category of spending was unusually large because of spending related to the COVID-19 pandemic.
  • In 2019, $28 billion was spent on UI, but in 2020, more than $173 billion was spent on UI.

Deficits

  • Deficit: the annual difference between federal spending and revenues.
  • The national debt is the total amount of money owed by the federal government at a point in time. It is an accumulation of all past borrowing.
  • The deficit is the annual difference between federal spending and revenues.
  • When spending is greater than revenues, the government must borrow to make up the deficit, or difference.

The National Debt

  • In 2025, the U.S. national debt was around $38 trillion.
  • Quite a bit of this debt is held by other branches of the federal government.
  • A better measure of the current debt is the amount of the national debt held by the public.
  • As of 2025, the national debt held by the public is nearly $30 trillion.
  • GDP is about $30 trillion, so the debt-to-GDP ratio is approximately 100%.

Interest on the National Debt

  • Every year, the government must pay interest to bondholders who lend it money.
  • Recently, the federal interest costs have amounted to nearly one trillion dollars a year.
ECON 104
30

Takeaways

  • Individual income taxes and Social Security and Medicare taxes bring in more than 80% of federal revenue.
  • The income tax is progressive: the marginal rate rises with income, and the average rate is below the marginal rate.
  • Social Security, Medicare, and Medicaid are the largest spending programs; defense and interest on the debt are also large.
  • The deficit is the annual gap between spending and revenues; the national debt is all past borrowing added up.
