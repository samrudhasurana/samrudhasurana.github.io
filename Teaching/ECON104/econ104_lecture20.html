The Federal Reserve

  • The Federal Reserve is a central bank.
  • The Federal Reserve creates money
    • It can print money.
    • It can electronically increase the reserves held by banks at the Fed.
  • There are 12 Federal Reserve Banks in the U.S.
ECON 104

The Fed as the Banker’s Bank

Deposits with the Fed
  • All major banks have bank accounts at the Federal Reserve (the Fed).
  • When Bank A makes a payment to Bank B, it authorizes the Fed to deduct the money from its account at the Fed and credit Bank B’s account at the Fed.
  • Bank accounts at the Fed are often called total reserves and play an important role in the financial system.
ECON 104

The Fed as the Banker’s Bank

Borrowing from the Fed
  • The banks also regularly borrow money from the Fed.
  • It is the lender of last resort — when no bank wants to lend to a bank, they can borrow from the Fed!
  • The interest charged by the Fed is a key instrument of monetary policy.

The Fed as the Government’s Bank

  • The U.S. Treasury has a bank account at the Fed.
  • This is where all the taxes and borrowings go.
  • The Fed manages borrowing of the government: sale of U.S. Treasury bonds.

How Does the Fed Influence Aggregate Demand?

It affects spending through the money supply!

  1. Open market operations of government bonds (buying and selling government bonds)
  2. Payment of interest on reserves
ECON 104
11

Open Market Operations

The Federal Reserve sells bonds

  • As the Federal Reserve sells a bond, the money supply falls (as loans fall) and the supply of loanable funds decreases.
  • What happens to the interest rate? It increases!
ECON 104
12

Open Market Operations

The Federal Reserve buys bonds

  • As the Federal Reserve buys a bond, the money supply rises (as loans rise) and the supply of loanable funds increases.
  • What happens to the interest rate? It decreases!
ECON 104
13

Open Market Operations: Summary

  • To increase money supply, and thus aggregate demand, the Fed buys bonds from banks. This increased money supply increases the supply of loanable funds, which lowers interest rates.
  • To decrease money supply, and thus aggregate demand, the Fed sells bonds to banks. This decreased money supply decreases the supply of loanable funds, which raises interest rates.
ECON 104
14

Payment of Interest on Reserves

  • This is basically the bank earning interest on its deposits with the Fed.
  • The Fed can choose the interest rate that it pays on reserves to influence lending and aggregate demand:
    • If the Fed raises the rate on reserves, banks hold more reserves and make fewer loans, thus decreasing aggregate demand.
    • If the Fed lowers the rate on reserves, banks hold less reserves and make more loans, thus increasing aggregate demand.

Lender of Last Resort

  • Under certain conditions, depositors may panic and then all try to withdraw their money at once, causing a bank run.
  • That’s where deposit insurance and the Federal Reserve come in.

Remember the Short Run vs. Long Run!

  • Remember that money is neutral in the long run — that neutrality includes real interest rates.
  • Also recall that an increase in aggregate demand increases the real growth rate only temporarily.
  • The Fed’s limited short-run influence over real interest rates and the long-run neutrality of aggregate demand both reflect the same principle: in the long run, money is neutral — it determines prices, not real economic activity.
ECON 104

Who Controls the Fed?

  • The Fed has a seven-member Board of Governors who are appointed by the president for 14-year terms and confirmed by the Senate.
  • The chairperson of the Fed is appointed by the president from among the members of the Board of Governors and confirmed by the Senate for a term of four years.
  • The Fed is made up of 12 Federal Reserve Banks, each headquartered in a different region of the country.

Takeaways

  • Money is a generally accepted medium of exchange. Banks create money by lending out their deposits.
  • The Fed is the central bank: the banker’s bank, the government’s bank, and the lender of last resort.
  • The Fed influences aggregate demand through the money supply: buying bonds increases it and lowers interest rates; selling bonds decreases it and raises interest rates.
  • The Fed can also influence lending by changing the interest rate it pays on reserves.
  • Money is neutral in the long run, so an increase in aggregate demand raises the real growth rate only temporarily.
