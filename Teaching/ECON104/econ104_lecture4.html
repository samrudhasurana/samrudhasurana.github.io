Law of Demand

Law of Demand

All else equal, when the price of a good increases, the quantity demanded of that good decreases.

And when the price of a good decreases, the quantity demanded of that good increases!

Why the downward sloping demand curve?

  • The first quantity is used to satisfy the most valuable need.
  • The Diet Coke that I drink along with my Biryani is more important to me than a Diet Coke I drink just to quench my thirst.
  • As we consume more, we allocate them towards less valued uses.
ECON 104
11

Demand as the Ability and Willingness to Pay

  • We all have wants.
  • Wants become demand with the ability and willingness to pay.
  • For my 2nd Diet Coke, which I will allocate to consuming with my Biryani, I am willing to pay up to $5.
  • For my 6th Diet Coke, which I will allocate towards my thirst, I am willing to pay no more than $3.
  • After my 12th Diet Coke, I would rather not pay, because I can consume water instead.

Breaking it down

Income Effect

  • As price increases, our money stretches less far
  • For instance, assume my Diet Coke budget is $20 for the week. If the price is $2, I cannot consume more than 10. If the price is $5, I cannot consume more than 4.

Substitution Effect

  • As price of a good increases too much, we might shift to substitutes.
  • I LOVE Diet Coke over Pepsi Black, but if I cannot find a cheap enough Diet Coke, I may switch to Pepsi Black

Consumer surplus

  • We noted that a buyer enters into an exchange only if they expect to benefit.
  • How do we model how much they benefit?
Consumer surplus

The difference between the maximum willingness to pay and the price actually paid, for all units consumed.

Consumer surplus

  • Difference between the maximum willingness to pay for each unit consumed, and the actual price paid.
  • Consumer surplus is the consumer’s gain from exchange
  • It is the area under the demand curve, and above the price line.

Change in Income

Normal Goods

  • For most goods, if your income increases, your demand for them will increase (and vice versa).

Inferior Goods

  • For a few goods, if your income increases, your demand for them will decrease (and vice versa).

Inferior Goods

A classic inferior-good example: as income rises, people buy less instant ramen, not more.
Inferiority is not a function of inherent qualities of the good, but only in relation to the person’s income and available substitutes.

Changes in price of related goods

Complements

Goods that are consumed alongside each other.

  • If the price of a complement increases, demand for the original good decreases (shifts left)
  • If the price of a complement decreases, demand for the original good increases (shifts right)
  • If the price of phones decreases, quantity demanded of phones will increase… and demand for internet at every price will also increase (demand curve shifts right for internet)
  • Other examples: peanut butter and jelly, printers and ink cartridges, cars and gasoline.
ECON 104
28

Changes in price of related goods

Substitutes

Goods used in place of each other.

  • If the price of a substitute rises, demand for the original good increases (shifts right)
  • If the price of a substitute falls, demand for the original good decreases (shifts left)
  • If the price of Diet Coke becomes 3x as much as Pepsi Black, which are you more likely to consume more of?
  • Other examples: butter and margarine, tea and coffee, Uber and Lyft.

Changes in tastes

  • Basically… preferences!
  • Preferences can change for many reasons. Think of fashion “fads”
  • If preference for a good increases, demand shifts right. And vice-versa.

Review

  • The law of demand: all else equal, if the price of a good increases, the quantity demanded of that good decreases.
  • Demand curves slope down! Water runs downhill!
  • Consumer surplus is the gain from trade on the consumer side.
  • Shifts in the demand curve occur when there is a change in tastes, incomes, etc – increases/decreases the quantity demanded at the same price.
  • Or alternatively, makes people willing to pay a higher or lower price for the same quantity
