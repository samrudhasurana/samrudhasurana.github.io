What Is the Price Telling Us?

Prices aren’t just numbers — they’re signals about scarcity.

↑ Price Rises

Demand increases  or  supply decreases

  • The good is becoming more scarce or more valuable.
  • The rising price communicates that to buyers and sellers.
↓ Price Falls

Demand decreases  or  supply increases

  • The good is becoming less scarce or less valuable.
  • The falling price communicates that to buyers and sellers.

Price Ceiling

Price ceiling

A government-mandated maximum price that can be charged for a good or service.

Remember: you can’t go higher than the ceiling!
ECON 104
05

Price Ceiling, cont.

  • A price ceiling is only effective if it is below the market price.
  • The lower-than-equilibrium price has two effects:
    • Quantity demanded is high.
    • Quantity supplied is low.
ECON 104
06

Graphically

A price ceiling set below the equilibrium price creates a shortage: quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied.
ECON 104
07

Price Ceilings: Consequences

  • Price ceilings cause shortages.
  • Reductions in quality:
    • Sellers can’t raise prices, so they lower quality instead.
    • When prices are held below market-clearing levels, quality falls.
ECON 104
08

Price Ceilings: Consequences, cont.

  • Buyers have to compete with each other in various ways:
    • Bribes
    • Waiting
  • At least the seller gets the money from a bribe. Nobody gets the time spent waiting in line.
ECON 104
09

Price Ceilings: Consequences, cont.

The gap between willingness to pay and the ceiling price becomes wasted time, not savings.

Price Ceilings: Misallocation of Resources

  • Buyers with a high willingness to pay are prevented from signaling this to producers by offering to pay more.
  • As a result, low-valued uses are served just as much as high-valued uses.
  • Price signals — which incentivize producers to serve the highest-valued uses first — no longer function.
ECON 104
12

Example: Rent Control

  • Housing shortages
    • Demand exceeds supply.
  • Deterioration in quality.
  • Investors stop building apartment complexes (further exacerbates the shortage).
  • A black market develops.
  • Misallocation of resources.

Price Floor

Price floor

A minimum legal price for a good or service, set above the equilibrium price.

Think: the floor keeps you from falling.
ECON 104
14

Graphically

A price floor set above the equilibrium price creates a surplus: quantity supplied exceeds quantity demanded.
ECON 104
15

Price Floor, cont.

  • Direct effect: a surplus — sellers will want to supply a larger quantity than buyers are willing to purchase.
  • Indirect effects:
    • The quantity exchanged will fall.
    • Fewer gains from trade.

Does the Minimum Wage Help the Poor?

  • While increasing the minimum wage will increase the wages of low-skill workers, their on-the-job training opportunities, non-wage benefits, working conditions, and employment will decline.
    • Benefits higher-skilled workers at the expense of lower-skilled workers.

Empirical Data

  • Early studies looked at data from the 1970s and 1980s.
  • “…minimum wage policies had reduced employment of 14-15 year olds by 46%, by 27% for those 16-17, and by 15% for the 18-19 year-old group.”

Do Economists Agree?

  • Employment Policies Institute survey found:
    • About 75% of economists opposed a federal minimum wage of $15/hr.
    • Surveyed economists believe that a $15 minimum wage would:
      • Negatively affect youth employment (83%).
      • Negatively impact adult employment (52%).
      • Reduce the number of jobs available (76%).
ECON 104

Do Economists Agree?, cont.

  • 80% of economists believe a $15 minimum wage would result in employers hiring entry-level workers with greater skills.
  • 67% stated that it would be more difficult for small businesses (<50 people) to stay in business.

Interfering with the Price System

  1. Prices communicate information about relative scarcities and how much people value goods.
  2. When prices are controlled, that information stops being communicated — but it doesn’t eliminate the underlying change in reality.
Analogy: if the water is heating up and you remove the thermometer — does that remove the heat?

Again: scarcity implies competition. It can take different forms:

1

Destructive

Fighting over who gets the good.

2

Unproductive

Waiting in line — nobody benefits from the wait.

3

Productive

Exchanging in the market — both sides gain.

We want productive competition.

Conclusion

Price CeilingsA price fixed below the market price
  1. Shortages
  2. Lost gains from trade (deadweight loss)
  3. Wasteful decreases in quality
  4. Wasteful competition between buyers
Price FloorsA price fixed above the market price
  1. Surpluses
  2. Lost gains from trade (deadweight loss)
  3. Wasteful increases in quality
  4. Wasteful competition between sellers
