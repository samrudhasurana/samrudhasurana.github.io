Definitions

Gross Domestic Product (GDP)

The market value of all finished goods and services produced within a country in a year.

GDP per capita

GDP divided by population.

ECON 104
07

GDP Is the MARKET VALUE…

  • GDP is an economy’s total output. It includes millions of different goods and services.
  • It’s not completely true that we cannot compare apples to oranges — we do so every day — with the help of prices!
  • GDP uses market values to determine how much each good or service is worth, and then sums the total.
Worked example
Apples: $2 × 2 billion = $4 billion
Oranges: $3 × 1 billion = $3 billion
Total GDP
$4 billion + $3 billion = $7 billion
ECON 104
08

…of All FINISHED…

  • Intermediate goods are sold to firms and then bundled or processed with other goods or services for sale at a later stage.
  • Finished goods are sold to final users and then consumed or held in personal inventories.
  • To avoid double counting, only finished goods are included in GDP.
  • However, machinery and equipment used to produce other goods are included in GDP.
Are coffee beans used at Starbucks included in GDP?
No.
What if they are used at home?
Yes.
ECON 104
09

…GOODS AND SERVICES…

  • The output of an economy includes both goods and services.
  • Haircuts, medical care, and transportation are some examples of services.
  • Today, most of GDP is services.
  • In the United States, services account for nearly 80% of GDP.
ECON 104
10

…PRODUCED WITHIN A COUNTRY… (domestic)

  • GDP measures production, so sales of used goods, such as a used car, and financial assets do not add to GDP.
  • U.S. GDP includes goods and services produced by labor and capital located in the United States, regardless of the nationality of the workers or property owners.
If the coffee beans are imported from Ethiopia, are they included in U.S. GDP?
No.
ECON 104
11

…IN A YEAR.

  • National wealth refers to the value of a nation’s entire stock of assets.
  • GDP tells us how much the nation produced in a year, not how much it has accumulated in its entire history.

Nominal vs. Real GDP

Remember, we get GDP by multiplying the price of the goods by the quantity of those goods produced. Can you see problems that might arise when we use this equation to measure GDP growth over time?

ECON 104
14

Nominal GDP vs. Real GDP

  • We want to see how much production has increased, but we might only be measuring how much PRICES have increased!
    • If prices go up, we get a higher GDP even if production hasn’t increased at all!
    • We need to normalize prices to a single year to track real growth
ECON 104
15

Nominal GDP vs. Real GDP

Nominal GDP

Calculated using prices from the year the goods were produced.

Real GDP

Adjusting for price changes, by using the same set of prices in all periods.

Real GDP Growth per Capita

  • Growth in real GDP per capita is usually the best reflection of changing living standards.
  • Growth in real GDP does not account for changes in population.
  • There can be large differences for countries with rapidly growing populations.
Example: Ethiopia (IMF projection)
Real GDP growth: 9.2%  —  Population growth: 2.5%
Real GDP per capita growth
9.2% − 2.5% ≈ 6.7% — noticeably smaller than the headline number

The Many Ways of Splitting GDP

  • Economists split the production of goods and services in many different ways.
  • Two common ways:
    • National spending approach: Add up the components of spending.
      Y = C + I + G + (Exports − Imports)
    • Factor income approach: Add up the income generated by producing goods and services.
      Y = Employee Compensation + Rent + Interest + Profit

The Expenditure Approach

Y = C + I + G + NX

SymbolComponentDefinition
YNominal GDPThe market value of all finished goods and services.
CConsumptionThe market value of consumption goods and services.
IInvestmentThe market value of investment goods, also called capital goods.
GGovernment purchasesThe market value of government purchases.
NXNet exportsThe market value of exports minus the market value of imports.

Factor Income Approach

  • Y = Employee compensation + Rent + Interest + Profit
  • When a consumer spends money, the money is received by workers, landlords, owners of capital, and businesses.
  • We can therefore calculate GDP by adding up all of the incomes received.
  • When using the factor income approach, we need to make adjustments for things like sales taxes.

Problems with GDP

  • Illegal or underground transactions are omitted.
  • GDP does not account for non-priced production when valuable goods and services are produced, but no explicit monetary payment is made. For example: me doing my own dishes.
  • GDP does not account for the value of “bads” like pollution.
  • GDP does not account for health of nations, for instance, life expectancy.
ECON 104

Takeaways

  1. GDP is an estimate of the economic output of a nation over a year.
  2. GDP per capita is a rough estimate of the standard of living in a nation.
  3. Real GDP is corrected for inflation by using the same set of prices in every year.
  4. GDP can be summed up in different ways.
  5. The national spending identity splits GDP according to different classes of income spending: Y = C + I + G + NX.
  6. The factor income approach splits GDP into different classes of income receiving: Y = Employee compensation + Interest + Rent + Profit.
