Saving, Investment, and the Market for Loanable Funds

  • The interest rate is the price of funds.
  • Saving creates the supply of loanable funds. Borrowing is the demand for loanable funds.
  • At the equilibrium interest rate, the quantity supplied equals the quantity demanded.
  • Banks, bonds, and stocks connect savers and borrowers. A bond makes you a lender. A stock makes you a part owner.

Types of Unemployment

Natural unemployment rate = frictional + structural. True unemployment varies around this natural rate.

TypeWhat it isCauses
FrictionalShort-term unemployment caused by the difficulty of matching employee to employer.Costly information; new workers entering the workforce; changing careers. Not necessarily bad.
StructuralPersistent, long-term unemployment.Mismatch between skills workers have and skills employers want; unemployment benefits, minimum wage, unions, hiring and firing costs.
CyclicalUnemployment correlated with the ups and downs of the business cycle.When GDP is falling, firms tend to lay off workers; wages are “sticky.”

Cyclical Unemployment

  • During recessions, unemployment increases dramatically.
  • Unemployment is higher when real GDP growth is below average and lower when it is above average.
  • Wages are “sticky”: the price level is too low and the wage rate is too high.

What Is Money? The Money Supply Today

  • Money is a generally accepted medium of exchange. It is what you use to buy other goods.
  • Money supply = currency held by the public + deposits. Cash sitting in bank vaults (reserves) is not counted.
  • Deposits don’t create money; bank loans do: $200 + $60 + $10 = $270.

Money and Inflation: The Quantity Theory

Mv = PY, so in growth rates M + v = P + Y. When v and Y are stable, the growth rate of the money supply is approximately equal to the inflation rate.

Aggregate Demand and Long-Run Aggregate Supply

  • M + v = P + Y. Spending growth is split between inflation and real growth.
  • AD slopes downward. LRAS is vertical: capital, labor, and technology fix the potential growth rate.
  • Spending growth of 10% and LRAS at 3% means inflation of 7%.
Today: nominal GDP grew 6.3% over the year to 2026 Q2: 2.2% real growth plus about 4% inflation.

Real Shocks: Shifts in LRAS

Positive real shock: LRAS shifts right

Real GDP growth rate rises. Inflation rate falls. The same money spending chases more goods, so prices rise more slowly.

Negative real shock: LRAS shifts left

Real GDP growth rate falls. Inflation rate rises. The same money spending chases fewer goods, so prices rise more quickly.

ECON 104
17

An Increase in Aggregate Demand

  • Spending growth rises, and AD shifts right. Wages and prices are sticky, so real growth rises first: a to b (short run).
  • Employees demand higher wages, and costs rise. The SRAS curve shifts up and passes through c: b to c (long run).
  • In the long run, money is neutral. The increase is reflected entirely in a higher inflation rate.
ECON 104
18

A Fall in Aggregate Demand

  • Spending declines, but wages are sticky. Shopkeepers cut production and real growth declines: a recession (a to b, short run).
  • Eventually wages and costs become unstuck. The SRAS curve shifts down and passes through c: the same growth rate and a lower inflation rate (b to c, long run).
  • Examples: the 2008 financial crisis (AD left) and the COVID-19 stimulus (AD right, higher inflation).
