Robert E. Hall & Michael Woodford

What is the prize for?

Microfoundations of macroeconomics

Explaining big-picture outcomes like unemployment and inflation by adding up the decisions of individual households and businesses.

  • Why it matters: it helps central banks and governments even out the ups and downs of the economy.

Why did the old models fail?

  • Old Keynesian models relied on stable statistical relationships between big variables, like the Phillips curve: inflation is low when unemployment is high.
  • In the 1970s oil crises, inflation rose even as unemployment rose. The relationships broke down.
  • Policy gave too much stimulus at the wrong time: years of low growth and high inflation.
Robert Lucas: build macro models on microfoundations. Inflation, consumption, and production come from the decisions of millions of households and businesses.

Their big ideas

HALL

Spending follows expectations

Households spend based on what they expect to earn in the future. Their spending changes only when new news arrives (a promotion!), so changes can’t be predicted. About 80% of households behave this way; the rest live hand to mouth.

WOODFORD

Prices are sticky

Firms can’t reset prices every time demand or costs change. So when demand falls, prices don’t, and output and jobs fall. Firms also set prices by what they expect in the future.

WOODFORD

The interest rate is the lever

Central banks steer the economy through the interest rate, not the amount of money in circulation. Credible, clear communication about future rates matters.

Takeaways

  • Macro outcomes are the sum of millions of micro decisions: build models from the bottom up.
  • Expectations about the future drive both spending (Hall) and pricing (Woodford).
  • Their New Keynesian models are now used by central banks and finance ministries around the world.
  • The pandemic and energy crises did not turn into another 1930s depression or another 1970s.
